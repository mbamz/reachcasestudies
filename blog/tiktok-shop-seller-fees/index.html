TikTok Shop Seller Fees: What They Actually Cost at Scale

Every brand vetting TikTok Shop asks some version of the same question: what's the real take rate? The honest answer is that the platform fee is smaller and simpler than most brands expect — and the number that actually determines your margin at scale isn't published anywhere.

The question every brand asks before they sign

Every brand vetting TikTok Shop asks us some version of the same question in the first call: what does the platform actually take? It's a fair question, and it's the right one to ask before committing budget — but most of what's published online about TikTok Shop seller fees is either outdated, blended from other marketplaces' fee structures, or just wrong. We pulled the current numbers directly from TikTok Shop's own Seller Center documentation so this is a straight answer, not a guess.

Two things up front. First: the platform fee is smaller and simpler than most brands expect. Second: the number that actually determines your margin at scale isn't the platform fee at all — it's a fee TikTok doesn't set, and a spend line that's entirely yours to control. We'll get to both.

The referral fee: TikTok Shop's actual take rate

TikTok Shop's core platform charge is called the referral fee, and as of April 1, 2024, it's a flat 6% on qualified orders across most US product categories. That's the number to anchor on. It is not a tiered structure that climbs with volume, and it is not a category-by-category maze the way it can be on some other marketplaces.

The fee is calculated on a specific base, not the full order value: Referral Fee = Fee Rate × (Customer Payment + Platform Discount − Tax). Platform-funded discounts get included in that base even though TikTok reimburses the seller for them separately, so the fee base can look slightly larger than the amount that actually lands in your account.

There are real category exceptions, and they matter if any part of your catalog sits in them: select jewelry sub-categories (diamond, gold, jade, platinum/carat gold, and ruby/sapphire/emerald) dropped to 5% as of October 31, 2024, and select pre-owned and collectibles items carry a reduced 3% rate on the portion of a sale above $10,000. For a brand doing real volume in apparel, beauty, food and beverage, or wellness — the categories we see most — the flat 6% is the number that applies.

New sellers also get a temporary break: a discounted 3% referral fee for 30 days, triggered by a first sale within 60 days of completing onboarding. Worth knowing, but not the number to plan around if you're already doing volume and evaluating whether the platform's economics pencil out at your scale — that 30-day window closes fast, and the 6% rate is what you'll be operating under for the life of the account.

Refunds don't hand the fee back in full

When an order is refunded, TikTok doesn't simply return the referral fee it collected. It returns the fee minus a Refund Administration Fee — 20% of the original referral fee, capped at $5 per SKU as of May 15, 2025. For most brands this is a rounding error. For categories that run a higher return rate, it's a real line item worth modeling into your margin, not something to discover after the fact in a settlement report.

Operator note — Settlement to a seller's bank account happens after delivery with no active refund request, subject to a $1 minimum threshold per TikTok's own documentation. If your finance team is reconciling TikTok Shop payouts against order data, the refund window and that $1 threshold are the two places small discrepancies usually come from.

The fee TikTok doesn't publish a clean number for

Here's where we'd rather tell you plainly that we don't have a verified number than hand you one that looks precise and isn't. Public sources disagree, sometimes sharply, on whether TikTok Shop charges a separate payment processing fee on top of the 6% referral fee. Some describe the 6% as an all-in rate that already covers payment processing. Others cite an additional processing charge in the low single digits. We were not able to confirm a single, current, category-consistent figure for this from TikTok's own seller-facing documentation, and we're not going to publish someone else's guess as fact.

What we can tell you: this is exactly the kind of line item that varies by region, payout method, and account history, which is likely why it doesn't show up as one clean published rate the way the referral fee does. If payment processing cost matters to your margin model at your order volume, the accurate number is the one in your own Seller Center dashboard, not a percentage from a blog post — ours included.

Affiliate commission isn't a TikTok fee. It's yours to set

This is the point that gets lost most often when brands price out TikTok Shop: affiliate commission — what you pay creators for the sales they drive — is not a platform fee. TikTok doesn't set it, cap it at a fixed rate, or take a cut of it beyond the standard referral fee on the resulting sale. You set it, per product, per creator tier, and you can change it.

That makes affiliate commission the single biggest lever a brand controls in its TikTok Shop cost structure — and one of the few line items where negotiating well has an immediate, visible payoff. A health and wellness brand we work with turned one creator retainer into a 600% return by the three-week mark — not from better creative, but from the rate negotiated before the deal started. The lever wasn't TikTok's fee structure. It was ours to pull.

The same principle scales past a single creator deal. A supplements brand cleared a $30K monthly GMV goal ten days early, then kept climbing to $33K before the month closed, running a seeding and commission structure built to reward proven affiliates rather than pay everyone the same flat rate. Tiered commission costs more per top performer and less in aggregate waste — that trade is one TikTok's fee schedule has no say in.

Ad spend: a budget you set, not a fee TikTok charges you

GMV Max and other paid amplification on TikTok Shop work the same way: it's spend you allocate against a target, not a platform fee layered on top of the referral fee. TikTok doesn't charge sellers a fixed percentage to use GMV Max — you set a budget, and the referral fee still applies to whatever sales close, paid or organic.

A food and beverage brand we work with scaled daily ad budget up 125% over roughly ten days and cleared $10K in GMV in the first four days of June off the increased spend. The referral fee on those sales was the same flat 6% it would have been on an organic order. The cost that moved was the budget the brand chose to commit, not a rate TikTok imposed.

What the full stack actually costs at scale

Put together, a brand doing real volume on TikTok Shop is managing three separate cost lines, not one blended "TikTok fee":

  • The referral fee — a flat, published 6% on most categories (5% or 3% in narrow exceptions), TikTok's rate, non-negotiable.
  • Affiliate commission — the brand's rate, set per product and creator tier, the primary lever for controlling cost per sale.
  • Ad spend (GMV Max and Spark ads) — the brand's budget, allocated against a return target, not a fixed percentage of sales.

There's no dedicated case study we can point to that isolates "blended take rate" as a single proven number, because it isn't one fixed number — it's a function of a brand's own commission structure and ad allocation, which differ by category and by month. Anyone who quotes you one clean all-in percentage for TikTok Shop is rounding off the two variables that are actually within your control.

That's the honest shape of it. TikTok's own cut is smaller and more predictable than most brands assume walking in. What determines whether the platform's economics work at your scale is how well you run the two levers TikTok doesn't touch.
