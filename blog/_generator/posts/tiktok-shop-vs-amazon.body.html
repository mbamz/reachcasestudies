TikTok Shop vs. Amazon: an allocation call, not a rivalry

A brand clearing eight figures a year on Amazon asked us a version of this question last month: TikTok Shop vs. Amazon, where does the next $50,000 of marketing budget actually go? Not which platform is better. Not whether to leave Amazon — nobody serious is suggesting that. Just: for the next incremental dollar, which channel earns it back faster.

That's a different question than most "TikTok Shop vs. Amazon" content answers, and it's the one that matters once a brand is already doing real volume. We run TikTok Shop for brands in exactly that position, so here's the honest version: where Amazon still wins, where TikTok Shop is winning on unit economics right now, and the framework we actually use with clients to decide the split.

Where Amazon still wins

Amazon captures demand that already exists. A buyer searching a product term on Amazon has already decided to buy something in that category — the platform's job is just winning the click among competitors. That's a fundamentally easier conversion problem than TikTok Shop's, where most buyers weren't looking for anything until a video put the product in front of them.

Amazon also carries fulfillment and trust infrastructure most brands spent years building: FBA logistics, Prime delivery expectations, an established review base, and a buyer who already trusts the checkout. None of that transfers to TikTok Shop on day one. A brand's Amazon operation is a sunk asset that keeps paying rent. Don't treat "how much do we put into TikTok Shop" as a question that requires walking away from any of it.

Operator note — If a SKU's growth is capped by search volume, not by content or creator reach, that's an Amazon problem, and no amount of TikTok Shop spend fixes it. Match the platform to the actual constraint.

Where TikTok Shop wins on unit economics right now

The case for incremental TikTok Shop spend isn't about reach. It's that the channel is still early enough for a well-run account that a marginal dollar frequently returns more than it would chasing more expensive, more saturated Amazon keywords. Three real examples from accounts we run:

Reallocated budget outperformed new budget

A food and beverage brand we work with was already running TikTok Shop as a retail channel, not a social experiment, at a steady $111K a month. When a client-requested test on the account's ad mix didn't pan out, the team didn't ask for more budget — it reallocated the spend already inside the account, and the month closed at $161K, up 45% month over month. The lesson isn't "spend more." It's that the marginal return on TikTok Shop ad dollars, redeployed correctly, was still high enough to recover a failed test and clear the prior baseline by roughly $50K in the same month.

ROI went up as spend went up

An oral care brand running GMV Max on a flat daily budget doubled that budget from $500 to $1,125 a day into a promotional demand window. GMV Max output rose 111% week over week, and ROI on that spend didn't dip the way scaled paid spend usually does — it rose to 1.98, a 69% week-over-week improvement. On Amazon, incrementally scaling PPC into a saturated keyword set is the more common pattern: rising CPCs eating into ROAS as the budget grows. This account moved the opposite direction.

Affiliates are a cheaper acquisition lane than most brands assume

A personal care brand closed May at $36.8K in total shop GMV. After the team lifted a platform-level ceiling on affiliate outreach and redirected the new headroom to creators who'd already converted, June closed at $121.7K — a 3.3× jump — with affiliates driving $99.9K of it, 82% of shop volume, averaging $266 in affiliate GMV per product sample sent out. That's revenue earned through commission paid on completed sales, not upfront ad spend against uncertain conversion. It's a materially different cost structure than paid acquisition on either platform.

The allocation framework we use with 8- and 9-figure brands

None of the above means "move the budget." It means TikTok Shop earns a real, sized test before Amazon gets the next incremental dollar by default. The framework we run clients through:

  • Size the first tranche as a test, not a channel switch. Use whatever the brand already allocates to testing new channels quarterly — this isn't a bet-the-budget decision, it's a bet-the-test-budget decision.
  • Judge it on marginal GMV per dollar, not brand awareness. TikTok Shop and Amazon PPC should be compared on the same basis: what did the next dollar actually produce, not what it felt like it produced.
  • Compare affiliate CAC against Amazon PPC CPC on the same or adjacent SKUs. Commission-on-sale and cost-per-click are structurally different costs. Put them side by side for the categories that matter, not in the abstract, and price in what TikTok Shop takes in platform fees so the comparison is net, not gross.
  • Reallocate structurally once ROI holds or improves as spend scales. That's the signal in the oral care account above — efficiency going up with volume is the tell that the channel has headroom left, not the ceiling.
  • Keep Amazon as the demand-capture engine. Use TikTok Shop for demand creation and new-to-brand acquisition through affiliates and seeded content — buyers who weren't searching for the category until a creator put it in front of them.

What breaks if you get the split wrong

Overcorrecting is the more common mistake. A brand that pours a large lump sum into TikTok Shop without an affiliate bench or a seeding cadence already running is spending against a channel that isn't ready for it — GMV Max amplifies content that's already proven to convert, it doesn't manufacture proof from nothing. That's how a brand ends up with a disappointing TikTok Shop quarter and a false conclusion that "TikTok Shop doesn't work for us," when the real issue was sequencing.

The quieter mistake is staying at 100% Amazon by default. Every incremental dollar that goes to a fifth or sixth round of PPC bidding on the same saturated keywords is a dollar not testing a channel where, as the numbers above show, the marginal return can still be higher, and where new-to-brand customers are being won by whichever brand in the category shows up in their feed first.

The operator takeaway

TikTok Shop vs. Amazon was never a competition either platform needed to win outright. Amazon is where you keep capturing demand you already earned. TikTok Shop, run correctly, is where the next incremental dollar of growth budget increasingly does more work — not because Amazon got worse, but because a channel still building its ceiling usually returns more on the marginal dollar than one that's already found its plateau. The brands getting this right aren't picking a side. They're pricing both.
